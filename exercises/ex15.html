22  Exercises: Marginalizing the problem

TipConditional variables

Prerequisite for these exercises was covered within the session and is therefore not repeated here. The exercises build on this material.

22.1 Exercises:

A doctor or pharmaceutical company sends a request to the research team. You are responsible for translating that request into a statistical analysis plan. You must identify the estimand, decide which predictions or transformations are needed, and formulate a precise request to the statistician.

Below are discussion cases based specifically on sections 15.11.1–15.11.4.

A1. Does the average patient exist?

A pharmaceutical company asks:

We have developed a new treatment for stroke. We would like to know the probability of death for the average patient receiving the treatment.

The statistician proposes to calculate the predicted probability of death for a patient with:

  • Average age
  • Average BMI
  • Diabetes = balanced
  • Hypertension = balanced
  • Sex = balanced

Questions for discussion

  1. Does this describe a clinically meaningful patient?
  2. What does it mean for our study that we balance diabetes?
  3. What is the difference between predicting the outcome for an average patient and averaging predictions across all patients?
  4. Imagine that the population consists of two distinct groups: young patients with low mortality and older patients with high mortality. How does that affect your response on question 3?
Click for the solution
  1. No.
  2. 50% of stroke population has diabetes — which is probably not correct.
  3. Briefly:
  • predicting the outcome for an average patient: represents one imaginary profile’s estimate.
  • averaging predictions across all patients: the average effect for all profiles in our sample.
  1. The average person is now useless. The average effect is still useful, but we should consider and interaction effect.

A2: The company wants a risk ratio, not an odds ratio

The statistician in your company has made a statistically correct and meaningful analysis on your dataset. You receive a Table 2 of the outcomes:

The company asks:

We need to communicate the relative reduction in mortality risk associated with our treatment. Please report the risk ratio rather than the odds ratio.

The statistician explains that the correct estimator is logistic regression.

Questions:

  1. What is the coefficients reporting?
  2. What does exponentiating do?
  3. Will it be possible to give the company the effect measure they want with the estimator the statistician chose?
  4. The statistician proposes to use marginal effects to get the probability of mortality for each group. Suppose the model produces the following average predicted probabilities:
  • Treatment A: 20% mortality.
  • Treatment B: 30% mortality.

Calculate: the Risk difference and risk ratio.

Click for the solution
  1. log-odds ratios (log-OR)
  2. Odds ratios (OR)
  3. Yes, we can compute relative risk (RR) using marginal effects.
  4. RD = -10%. RR = 0.66

A3. The doctor wants to know whether treatment works

A doctor contacts the research team:

We have followed 500 patients with hypertension for 12 weeks. Some received Drug A and others received Drug B. The company wants to know whether Drug A is better than drug be on patients hypertension af 12 weeks of taking the drug.

The available variables are:

  • Treatment (A/B)
  • Age
  • Sex
  • Baseline systolic blood pressure (before drug initiation)
  • Systolic blood pressure at 12 weeks (after taking the drug)
  • BMI
  • Presence of hypertension (yes/no)

Your tasks:

  1. What is the scientific research question?
  2. What is the target population?
  3. What exactly do we want to estimate?
  4. What effect measure do you recommend?
  5. Write a regression equation that could address the question.
  6. Which variables would you consider adjusting for, and why?
Click for the solution
  1. We want to know whether Drug A results in a greater > reduction in systolic blood pressure after 12 weeks.
  2. Only patients with hypertension
  3. The effect of the drug for all patients with hypertension.
  4. Maybe a difference in mean blood pressure at 12 weeks or a difference in mean change from baseline? Notice that they mean quite different things!
  5. Depends on you choice…
  6. This is a medical question…

A2. The doctor wants to know whether age modifies treatment effect

A doctor suspects that Drug A works better in younger patients than in older patients.

The research team has data on:

  • Treatment (A/B)
  • Age
  • Baseline disease severity
  • Outcome: death (yes/no)

The doctor asks:

Can you tell me whether Drug A is more effective in younger patients?

Your tasks:

  1. How do we get information from younger and older patients with our dataset?
  2. Write a regression equation including an interaction term.
  3. What does the interaction coefficient tell us?
  4. Does the interaction coefficient directly tell us the treatment effect in younger patients?
  5. What estimates should the statistician report to make the results clinically meaningful?
Click for the solution
  1. There is, to my knowledge, no good way to split age in groups. Keep it continuous.
  2. death ~ treatment + age + treatment:age + baseline_disease_severity
  3. For each additional lifetime year (age), how does the treatment effect alter odds of survival.
  4. No, that’s a post-hoc calculation and we don’t get incertanties.
  5. Probability for death in a specified age-group, e.g., 30-40 and 65-75 and compare them directly.
