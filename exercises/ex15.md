### A: Let's start by considering what a regression coefficient tells us.

**Exercise 1A**: Regression coefficients versus scientific questions

Consider the following logistic regression model:

$$
  Y_i \sim Binomial(n_t, p_i)\\
  logit(p_i) = β_0, β_1X_i
$$

Suppose that β 1 = 1.2 .

Answer the following:

1. What scale is β 1 expressed on?
2. What happens to the log-odds when X increases by one unit?
3. Is it correct to say that the probability increases by 1.2?
4. Why is the effect of a one-unit increase in X on probability not necessarily
   constant across the range of X ?
5. What quantity would you ideally like to report to a clinician or researcher
   if the scientific question is about probability?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>

1. The coefficient is expressed on the log-odds scale.
2. The log-odds increase by 1.2 for a one-unit increase in *X*.
3. No. The coefficient is not a change in probability.
4. The logistic transformation is nonlinear. The same change in log-odds can correspond to very different changes in probability depending on the starting probability.
5. A change in predicted probability, or a probability-scale contrast, is usually easier to interpret.
</details>

**Exercise 2A:** The same coefficient, different probability changes

Consider two individuals with predicted probabilities of 0.10 and 0.50.

Suppose the predictor increases by one unit and the corresponding change in
log-odds is 1.

1. Calculate the new probability for the individual whose starting probability
   is 0.10.
2. Calculate the new probability for the individual whose starting probability
   is 0.50.
3. Calculate the change in probability for each individual.
4. What does this demonstrate about interpreting a logistic regression
   coefficient?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>

The inverse-logit transformation is:

$$
p=\frac{exp(η)}{1+exp⁡(η)}
$$

For a starting probability of 0.10:


$$
logit(0.10)=log(\frac{0.10}{0.90})
$$

After increasing the log-odds by 1, the probability becomes approximately 0.232.

The change in probability is therefore approximately:

0.232−0.10=0.132

or a 13.2 percentage-point increase.

For a starting probability of 0.50:

logit(0.50)=0

After increasing the log-odds by 1:

$$
p=\frac{exp⁡(1)}{1+exp⁡(1)}≈0.731
$$
The change in probability is therefore approximately:

$0.731−0.500=0.231$

or a 23.1 percentage-point increase.

The same change in log-odds can therefore produce different changes in probability.

</details>

Calculate the observed mortality proportion at each dose.

Which dose has the highest observed mortality?

Exercise 4B: Fit a logistic regression model We can model the number of deaths
using a binomial model.

Fit the following model:

d i e d i ∼ Binomial ( n i , p i )

with

logit ( p i ) = β 0 + β 1 d o s e i

Fit the model in R.

What does the coefficient for dose represent?

Is it a change in mortality probability?

Exponentiate the coefficient. What does the resulting quantity represent?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>

```{r}
#| label: 4b
BeetleMortality <- BeetleMortality |> mutate(not_dead=n-died)

m4b <- glm(
  cbind(died, not_dead) ~ dose,
  family = binomial(link = "logit"),
  data = BeetleMortality
)

m4b |>
  parameters::parameters(exponentiate = TRUE)
```

The coefficient for dose is a change in log-odds for a one-unit increase in dose.

After exponentiation, it becomes an odds ratio.

Neither quantity is a change in probability.

</details>

**Exercise 5B:** Ask the scientific question Suppose a researcher asks:

> "How much does the probability of beetle mortality change when dose increases
> by one unit?"

The regression coefficient does not directly answer this question.

Explain why.

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The logistic model describes a linear relationship on the log-odds scale:

$logit(p)=β0+β1dose$

But probability is obtained by applying the inverse-logit transformation.

Because the inverse-logit transformation is nonlinear, a one-unit increase in dose does not correspond to the same change in probability everywhere on the dose scale.

The probability-scale effect therefore depends on the value of dose.

</details>

C: Marginal slopes Exercise 6C: Calculate slopes across the dose range The
marginaleffects package can calculate slopes on the response scale.

```{r}
#| label: 6c

marginaleffects::slopes(
  m4b,
  variables = "dose",
  type = "response"
)
```

1. What scale are the reported slopes expressed on?
2. What does a slope represent here?
3. Why is calculating a slope on the response scale different from simply
   reporting the regression coefficient?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The slopes are calculated on the predicted probability scale.

A slope represents the instantaneous change in predicted mortality probability associated with a one-unit increase in dose at a particular value of dose.

Unlike the regression coefficient, the slope therefore describes a quantity directly related to probability.

Because the logistic transformation is nonlinear, the slope can differ depending on the value of dose.

</details>

**Exercise 7C:** Where is the effect largest? Calculate the marginal slope at
several clinically/scientifically meaningful doses.

For example:

```{r}
#| label: 7c

marginaleffects::slopes(
  m4b,
  variables = "dose",
  newdata = marginaleffects::datagrid(
    dose = c(1.70, 1.75, 1.80, 1.85)
  )
)

```

Answer:

1. At which dose is the increase in predicted mortality probability largest?
2. At which dose does the relationship begin to flatten?
3. Why would reporting only the logistic regression coefficient hide this
   information?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The marginal slope changes across the dose range.

The largest probability-scale change occurs in the region where the fitted logistic curve is steepest.

At low and high predicted probabilities, the curve becomes flatter, so the probability-scale change associated with a one-unit increase in dose becomes smaller.

The regression coefficient is constant because the model is linear on the log-odds scale. It therefore does not communicate the changing probability-scale effect across the dose range.

</details>

Exercise 8C: The average marginal slope Instead of choosing specific doses,
calculate the average marginal slope:

```{r}
#| label: 8c

marginaleffects::avg_slopes(
  m4b,
  variables = "dose"
)
```

1. What is being averaged?
2. What population does the average refer to?
3. How would you explain the resulting quantity to someone unfamiliar with
   marginal effects?
4. Rewrite the result in percentage-point units.

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The model calculates the probability-scale slope at each observation and then averages those slopes.

The average therefore represents the average change in predicted mortality probability associated with a one-unit increase in dose across the observed data.

The result can be expressed as a percentage-point change.

For example, if the estimate were 0.018, it would be described as:

A one-unit increase in dose was associated with an average 1.8 percentage-point increase in predicted mortality probability.

The exact estimate should be taken from the fitted model output rather than reported as a generic example.

</details>

D: A nonlinear dose-response relationship Exercise 9D: Allow the effect of dose
to change The previous model assumes that the relationship between dose and
log-odds is linear.

We can instead fit a quadratic model:

logit ( p i ) = β 0 + β 1 d o s e i + β 2 d o s e i 2

Fit the model:

```{r}
#| label: 9d

m9d <- glm( cbind(died, not_dead) ~ dose + I(dose^2), family = binomial(link =
"logit"), data = BeetleMortality )
```

1. Why does the quadratic term allow the relationship between dose and mortality
   to bend?
2. Can β 1 be interpreted as the change in mortality probability for a one-unit
   increase in dose?
3. Can β 1 alone tell us whether increasing dose always increases mortality?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
1. The quadratic term allows the linear predictor to curve as dose changes.
2. No. The coefficients are on the log-odds scale and the effect of dose depends on the value of dose.
3. No. In a quadratic model, the effect of dose depends on both dose and the two dose coefficients.
4. The derivative of the linear predictor with respect to dose is:

$\frac{dη}{ddose}=β1+2β2dose$

The corresponding probability-scale slope also depends on the predicted probability.

</details>

**Exercise 10D:** Calculate the marginal slopes from the quadratic model

Calculate the slopes across a grid of dose values.

```{r}
#| label: 10d

marginaleffects::slopes(
  m9d,
  variables = "dose",
  newdata = marginaleffects::datagrid(
    dose = seq(
      min(BeetleMortality$dose),
      max(BeetleMortality$dose),
      length.out = 100
    )
  )
)
```

1. Plot the estimated marginal slope against dose.
2. At what doses is the marginal effect positive?
3. Does the effect become smaller at higher doses?
4. Does the fitted model imply that increasing dose must always increase
   mortality?
5. What does a negative marginal slope at the highest doses mean?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The marginal slope is not constant across dose.

Because the model contains a quadratic term, the fitted relationship can flatten and eventually turn downward.

A negative marginal slope means that, according to the fitted model, increasing dose at that part of the dose range is associated with a decrease in predicted mortality probability.

This does not necessarily establish a biological mechanism. It describes the fitted relationship in the observed data and should be interpreted in light of the study design, uncertainty, and the range of doses observed.

</details>

**C???** From individuals to populations We now move to a different question.

Instead of asking:

"What is the effect of dose at this particular value?"

we will ask:

"What would the mortality rate look like if we changed the exposure for the
population?"

This is the central idea behind marginalizing a model.

G: Fit the Poisson model Exercise 12G: A model with smoking and age We will
model deaths using a Poisson regression with person-years as the exposure:

$deaths_i ∼ Poisson(λi)$
$log(λ_i) = β0 + β1smoking_i + β2age_i + log(personyears_i)$

Fit the model:

```{r}
#| label: 12g

m12g <- glm(
  deaths ~ smoking + age,
  family = poisson(link = "log"),
  offset = log(personyears),
  data = doctors
)
```

What does the coefficient for smoking represent?

Why is it not directly a difference in mortality rate?

What do we obtain if we exponentiate the smoking coefficient?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The smoking coefficient is a difference in log mortality rate, conditional on the other variables in the model.

Exponentiating it gives a rate ratio.

This is a model-based conditional comparison. It is not yet a population-average difference in mortality rate.

</details>

*H: Introduce the scientific question* **Exercise 13H:** What do we actually
want to know? Imagine that the scientific question is:

> "What would the mortality rate be if the population were smokers, compared
> with what it would be if the same population were non-smokers?"

This is a counterfactual comparison.

Write down the two hypothetical populations being compared.

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
We consider two hypothetical versions of the same population:

Everyone is assigned smoker.

Everyone is assigned non-smoker.

The observed ages are retained.

The comparison therefore asks how the predicted mortality rate changes when smoking status is changed while the age distribution of the population is held fixed.

</details>

**Exercise 14H:** Why age matters Suppose the model predicts different smoking
effects at different ages.

For example, imagine the model gives:

<!--panache-ignore-start-->
Age	Predicted rate if non-smoker	Predicted rate if smoker
1	0.0002	0.0010
2	0.0010	0.0040
3	0.0050	0.0120
4	0.0150	0.0300
5	0.0300	0.0500
<!--panache-ignore-end-->

A researcher asks:

> "What is the effect of smoking?"

Why is there no single answer until we specify which population we mean?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The smoking comparison differs across age.

An effect calculated entirely among age-1 observations answers a different question from an effect calculated among age-5 observations.

A population-average effect requires us to specify how the age-specific effects are combined.

This is the key idea behind marginalization.

</details>

Allow the smoking effect to vary by age

**Exercise 15I: Add an interaction Fit the model:**

$log(λ_i) = β0 + β1smoking_i + β2age_i + β3(smoking_i × age_i) + log⁡(personyears_i)$

```{r}
#| label: 15i

m15i <- glm(
  deaths ~ smoking * age,
  family = poisson(link = "log"),
  offset = log(personyears),
  data = doctors
)
```

What does the interaction allow the model to do?

Can the coefficient for smoking now be interpreted as the smoking effect for
every age?

What does the smoking coefficient represent when age = 0?

Why is this another situation where marginal effects are useful?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The interaction allows the association between smoking and mortality rate to vary with age.

The smoking coefficient alone is no longer the smoking comparison at every age.

Because the model uses an interaction, the smoking effect depends on age.

The smoking coefficient corresponds to the smoking comparison at the reference value of age.

Marginal effects allow us to calculate the smoking comparison at specific ages or average it over a population.

</details>

**Exercise 16J:** Calculate age-specific comparisons Use slopes() to calculate
the smoking comparison at each age.

```{r}
#| label: 16j

marginaleffects::slopes(
  m15i,
  variables = "smoking",
  newdata = marginaleffects::datagrid(
    age = 1:5
  )
)
```

1. What quantity is being estimated?
2. How does the smoking comparison change with age?
3. Why is this more informative than reporting the interaction coefficient
   alone?
4. How would you communicate one of the age-specific results to a clinician?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The analysis estimates the change in predicted mortality rate when smoking status changes, evaluated separately at each age.

The effect can therefore be communicated in the units of mortality rate rather than log-rate.

For example:

> At age category 4, changing smoking status from non-smoker to smoker was associated with an estimated increase of X deaths per 1,000 person-years.

The exact value should be obtained from the fitted model.

</details>

K: Marginalizing over the population **Exercise 17K**: Average the smoking
comparison

Now ask a population-level question:

"What is the average difference in predicted mortality rate if smoking status
were changed for the population?"

Use:

```{r}
#| label: 17k

marginaleffects::avg_comparisons(
  m15i,
  variables = "smoking"
)
```

Answer:

What is being averaged?

Over which observations is the averaging performed?

What does the resulting estimate represent?

How is this different from the smoking coefficient in the regression table?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The model first calculates the smoking comparison for each observation and then averages those comparisons.

The resulting estimate is therefore a population-average comparison over the observations used to define the averaging population.

It answers a counterfactual question of the form:

What would the average predicted mortality rate be if smoking status were changed from one level to the other for the population?

This differs from the regression coefficient because the coefficient is expressed on the log-rate scale and, in the presence of an interaction, represents the smoking effect at the reference value of age.

</details>

L: What population are we averaging over? Exercise 18L: Marginalizing over age
The phrase "average effect" can hide an important question:

Average over which population?

Suppose we want to estimate the smoking comparison for a population in which the
five age groups are equally represented.

Create an age grid:

```{r}
#| label: 18l

marginaleffects::datagrid(
  model = m15i,
  age = 1:5
)
```

Now calculate the smoking comparisons over this grid.

```{r}
#| label: 18l-comparison

marginaleffects::avg_comparisons(
  m15i,
  variables = "smoking",
  newdata = marginaleffects::datagrid(
    age = 1:5
  )
)
```

What population does this comparison describe?

How does it differ from the comparison in Exercise 17K?

Why can two marginal effects from the same model be different?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The age grid specifies the population over which the predictions are evaluated.

When the five age categories are represented equally, the marginal comparison gives equal weight to the five age-specific comparisons.

The comparison in Exercise 17K instead averages over the observations defining the default prediction population.

Two marginal effects can therefore differ because they answer questions about different populations.

The model has not changed. The target population has changed.

</details>

M: Counterfactual predictions **Exercise 19M:** Predict two counterfactual
populations Instead of directly calculating a comparison, calculate predicted
mortality rates under two scenarios:

Everyone is a smoker.

Everyone is a non-smoker.

Use avg_predictions() with appropriate newdata.

```{r}
#| label: 19m

marginaleffects::avg_predictions(
  m15i,
  newdata = datagrid(
    model = m15i,
    smoking = c("smoker", "non-smoker")
  )
)
```

1. What does each predicted value represent?
2. What happens if you subtract the non-smoker prediction from the smoker
   prediction?
3. How is this related to avg_comparisons()?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
Each prediction represents the average predicted mortality rate under one counterfactual smoking scenario.

The difference between the two predictions is a population-average difference in predicted mortality rate.

This is closely related to the comparison produced by avg_comparisons().

The two approaches emphasize different ways of expressing the same scientific idea:

avg_predictions() gives the predicted outcomes under each scenario.

avg_comparisons() directly gives the contrast between the scenarios.

</details>

N: Standardization and target populations **Exercise 20N:** Who is the target
population? Consider the following three questions.

> Question A What is the smoking comparison for the average observed population?

> Question B What is the smoking comparison for a population with equal numbers
> of people in each age category?

> Question C What is the smoking comparison among the observed smokers?

For each question:

1. What population should be used for marginalization?
2. Would you expect the three estimates to be identical?
3. Why or why not?

<details> <summary>**Click for the solution**</summary>
The target population differs between the questions.

Question A uses the observed population.

Question B uses a specified population with an equal distribution across age categories.

Question C targets the smoker population.

The estimates need not be identical because the age distributions differ between these populations and the smoking effect varies with age.

This illustrates an important principle:

A marginal effect is always marginal over something.

The target population should therefore be stated explicitly.

</details>
