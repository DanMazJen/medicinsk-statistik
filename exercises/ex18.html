23  Exercises: Evaluating a crystal ball

TipConditional variables

Prerequisite for these exercises was covered within the session and is therefore not repeated here. The exercises build on this material.

23.1 Exercises:

NB! These exercises are different from the rest of the course. They are conceptual and are best discussed with a friend or in groups.

In your future job as a medIS specialist, doctors, researchers, and pharmaceutical companies will ask you whether a prediction model is good enough to use in clinical practice.

Your responsibility is to translate their requests into statistical questions. You must identify what they actually want to know, which validation measure is appropriate, and what conclusions can and cannot be drawn from the results.

Remember: A model can perform well according to one measure and poorly according to another. There is no single number that tells us whether a prediction model is good.

A1. The hospital wants a highly accurate model

A researcher from Phraut University has developed a prediction model to identify patients at risk of developing sepsis within 48 hours of admission.

The model has been validated on 1,000 patients:

  • 950 patients did not develop sepsis.
  • 50 patients developed sepsis.

The model predicts that every patient will not develop sepsis.

The researcher reports:

My model has an accuracy of 95%. This is an excellent result, and we recommend implementing the model in clinical practice.

Questions for discussion

  1. How can the model achieve 95% accuracy without identifying a single patient with sepsis?
  2. Does accuracy tell us whether the model is useful for identifying patients with sepsis?
  3. What happens if the prevalence of sepsis changes from 5% to 30%?
  4. Which additional performance measures would you request from the statistician?
  5. Imagine you are responsible for deciding whether to implement this model. Would you accept the researchers’s recommendation?
Click for the solution
  1. The 5% inccorect are all the patients…
  2. No.
  3. Then, accuracy becomes 70%.
  4. Sensitivity (or positive predictive value)
  5. Hell no!

A2. The doctor wants to avoid false alarms

A doctor contacts you:

We have developed a prediction model for identifying patients who will develop acute kidney injury during hospital admission. We want to make sure that the model does not generate too many false alarms. Otherwise, we will unnecessarily perform additional examinations on healthy patients.

The statistician provides the following results:

Measure Model A Model B
Sensitivity 90% 65%
Specificity 70% 95%
Accuracy 75% 85%

The doctor asks you which model should be implemented.

Questions for discussion

  1. What does specificity measure?
  2. Which model is better at avoiding false positives?
  3. Which model is better at identifying patients who actually develop kidney injury?
  4. Why does Model B have higher accuracy?
  5. Can you recommend Model B based on these results alone?
  6. What additional information would you need before making a recommendation?
Click for the solution
  1. Proportion of false positives
  2. B
  3. A
  4. Overall more correct in classifying both positive and negative cases.
  5. No! We need ABCD framework. We have non right now…
  6. Calibration, discrimination, and decision curve analysis.

A3. The pharmaceutical company wants to know which model is best

Two research teams have developed prediction models for one-year mortality following stroke.

The pharmaceutical company asks:

We want to know which model is best at distinguishing between patients who will die and patients who will survive. We do not want to choose a specific classification threshold yet.

The statistician reports:

Measure Model A Model B
Sensitivity 80% 65%
Specificity 90% 95%
Accuracy 85% 89%
AUC 0.91 0.78

The company is confused because Model B has higher accuracy, but Model A has higher AUC.

Questions for discussion

  1. What does AUC measure?
  2. What does an AUC of 0.91 tell us?
  3. Why can Model B have higher accuracy but lower AUC?
  4. Does AUC depend on the classification threshold?
  5. Can a model with an AUC of 0.90 still make poor predictions?
  6. Which model would you recommend if the only objective is to distinguish between patients who die and patients who survive?
;
Click for the solution
  1. AUC in binary prediction is discrimination: The probability that the model will correctly assign higher risk to an individual with a disease than an individual without the disease, if it randomly drew to pairs from the binary outcome groups.
  2. 91% of the time, that model would correctly assign higher probability for the sick person than the healthy control (but not necessarily put the correct label on them!).
  3. Overall, assigns the correct label more often. But this could be due to misbalance in the data (similar to exercise A1).
  4. No! It’s every possible threshold applied.
  5. Absolutely! That is a questions that should be answered with calibration!
  6. AUC.

A4. The doctor wants probabilities that can be trusted

A hospital has developed a model to predict the probability of death within 30 days after surgery.

The doctor explains:

We want to use the predicted probabilities to communicate the risk of death to patients and to plan postoperative care. If the model predicts a 20% risk, we want that prediction to mean something clinically.

The statistician evaluates the model and reports the following results:

Measure Model A Model B
AUC 0.90 0.78
Calibration-in-the-large (CITL) -0.80 0.02
Calibration slope 0.65 1.02

Questions for discussion

  1. What does calibration measure, and why is it important when communicating predicted probabilities to patients?
  2. Model A has a CITL of -0.80. What does this tell us about its predictions? Is it systematically overestimating or underestimating mortality?
  3. Model B has a CITL close to zero. Does this mean that Model B is perfectly calibrated?
  4. What is the difference between calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope?
  5. Model A has a calibration slope of 0.65. What does this tell us about its predictions? Are the predicted risks too extreme or not extreme enough?
  6. Which model would you prefer if the purpose is to communicate individual mortality risks to patients?
  7. Can you conclude that Model B is the better prediction model overall?
Click for the solution
  1. Calibration is whether the predictions can be interpreted as probabilities. If this is not the case, they have no interpretable clinical meaning.
  2. Overestimating.
  3. No - on average it is, but any value could be wrong systematically!
  4. CITL assess marginal prediction. Calibration slope assess every predictions’ correspondence to actual probability.
  5. Predicted risks too extreme.
  6. B!
  7. No, we need a decision curve analysis if the model should be used clincally. But for a statistical model that would not have any cost/ethical decisions, this is actually sufficient.
