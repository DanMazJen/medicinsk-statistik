23  Exercises: Evaluating a crystal ball

23.1 Learning objectives

The learning objectives for this session are:

  • Udvise forståelse for usikkerheds- og sandsynlighedsbegrebet samt grundliggende begreber indenfor biostatistik
  • Udvise kendskab til de grundliggende overordnede studietyper samt at skelne mellem forklarende, eksplorative og prædiktive studier
  • Redegøre for forskellige typer af tilfældig og ikke tilfældig variation
  • Udvise forståelse for statistiske værktøjers begrænsninger og muligheder
  • Forstå statistiske problemstillinger, der er centrale for medicin med industrial specialisering og forstå, hvordan de biostatistiske værktøjer kan appliceres på disse problemstillinger

23.2 Exercises:

23.2.1 A: Reviewing other’s models

Exercise 1A: What does “validation” actually mean?

Imagine you have developed a binary classifier that predicts whether a patient will experience an event.

The development data show excellent performance.

Questions:

  • What exactly are you trying to establish when you validate the model?

  • Why isn’t a high accuracy/AUC on the development dataset sufficient?

  • What would make a validation dataset genuinely independent?

  • Give three different ways a model could appear to perform well while actually being poorly validated.

    Learning goal: Distinguish model development from assessment of generalizability.

Exercise 1B: Overfitting and generalizability

A model is developed in 1,000 patients. It achieves an AUC of 0.92 in the development dataset.

When the model is evaluated in a new dataset of 1,000 patients, the AUC is 0.71.

Questions:

  • What could explain the difference between the development and validation performance?
  • What is overfitting?
  • Why is development-set performance usually optimistic?
  • What is the difference between internal and external validation?
  • Why is performance in genuinely new observations more informative about generalizability?

Learning goal: Understand why validation is necessary and how overfitting can lead to overly optimistic estimates of model performance.

Exercise 2B: Understanding discrimination as ranking

Consider five patients with predicted probabilities:

Patient Predicted probability Observed outcome
A 0.90 1
B 0.75 1
C 0.60 0
D 0.30 0
E 0.10 0

Questions:

  • Does the model discriminate well in this example?
  • Which pairs of patients are correctly ranked?
  • What would perfect discrimination look like?
  • Could a model have excellent discrimination even if all of its predicted probabilities were systematically too high?
  • What aspect of prediction is the AUC measuring: the accuracy of the probabilities themselves, or the ability to rank patients?

Learning goal: Understand that discrimination is fundamentally about ranking individuals according to their risk.

Exercise 2A: AUC versus calibration

Consider two models:

Model AUC Calibration
A 0.86 Poor
B 0.76 Excellent

A colleague says:

“Model A is clearly better because its AUC is higher.”

Questions:

Do you agree?

  • What does the AUC tell you?
  • What does it not tell you?
  • Give a practical situation where Model B might be preferable.
  • Give a practical situation where Model A might still be useful.

Learning goal: Prevent the common mistake of treating AUC as a complete measure of predictive performance.

Exercise 3C: Calibration-in-the-large versus calibration slope

Consider four models evaluated in the same validation population:

Model Calibration intercept Calibration slope
A 0.00 1.00
B -1.00 1.00
C 0.00 0.50
D 0.50 1.40

Questions:

  • Which model has no evidence of systematic over- or underprediction in the overall level of risk?
  • Which model has predictions that are too extreme?
  • Which model has predictions that are not extreme enough?
  • What does a calibration intercept different from 0 indicate?
  • What does a calibration slope different from 1 indicate?
  • Can a model have a good calibration intercept but a poor calibration slope?
  • Can a model have a good calibration slope but a poor calibration intercept?

Learning goal: Distinguish problems with the overall level of predicted risk from problems with the spread or extremeness of predictions.

Exercise 3A: Calibration slope

Imagine fitting a logistic regression in the validation dataset where the original model’s linear predictor is the only predictor.

You obtain:

  • calibration intercept = 0.05
  • calibration slope = 0.62

Questions:

  • What does a slope of 1 represent?
  • What does a slope substantially below 1 suggest?
  • What would a slope substantially above 1 suggest?
  • Why might a slope below 1 be evidence that the original model’s predictions are too extreme?
  • How is this different from simply asking whether the average predicted risk equals the observed event rate?

This is closely aligned with the paper’s proposed “B” component of validation.

Exercise 3B: What does calibration actually mean?

Suppose a model is used to predict the probability of an event.

Among patients for whom the model predicts approximately 20% risk, 20% actually experience the event.

Among patients for whom the model predicts approximately 50% risk, 50% actually experience the event.

Among patients for whom the model predicts approximately 80% risk, 80% actually experience the event.

Exercise 4A: The threshold trap

Suppose a classifier outputs probabilities, but somebody converts them into classes:

Predict positive if probability ≥ 0.50; otherwise predict negative.

The resulting confusion matrix gives:

  • Accuracy = 90%
  • Sensitivity = 40%
  • Specificity = 98%

Questions:

  • Is this necessarily a bad classifier?
  • Why does the 0.50 threshold matter?
  • What happens if the event is rare?
  • Would changing the threshold change the AUC?
  • Would changing the threshold change sensitivity and specificity?
  • Is threshold-based classification appropriate if the model is intended to provide individual risk estimates?

Learning goal: Separate evaluation of probability predictions from evaluation of a particular classification threshold.

Exercise 4B: What happens when the classification threshold changes?

A model produces predicted probabilities for an event.

Consider three possible classification thresholds:

  • Threshold = 0.10
  • Threshold = 0.50
  • Threshold = 0.90

A patient is classified as positive whenever their predicted probability is greater than or equal to the chosen threshold.

Questions:

  • What would you expect to happen to the number of patients classified as positive as the threshold increases?
  • What would you expect to happen to sensitivity?
  • What would you expect to happen to specificity?
  • What would you expect to happen to false positives?
  • What would you expect to happen to false negatives?
  • Would changing the threshold change the underlying AUC?
  • Why can the same prediction model have very different sensitivity and specificity depending on the chosen threshold?

Learning goal: Understand that sensitivity and specificity depend on the classification threshold, whereas AUC evaluates discrimination across thresholds.

Exercise 5A: The prevalence shift thought experiment

A model is developed in Population X:

  • event prevalence = 30%

It is externally validated in Population Y:

  • event prevalence = 5%

Assume the relationship between predictors and outcome is unchanged.

Questions:

  • Which performance measures might change merely because prevalence changed?
  • Would you expect the AUC necessarily to collapse?
  • Would you expect the calibration intercept to change?
  • Why could a model retain useful ranking ability while producing systematically incorrect absolute risks?

Learning goal: Understand why external validation can reveal case-mix and population differences that aren’t captured by discrimination alone.

Questions:

  • What does this tell you about the calibration of the model?
  • What would perfect calibration look like?
  • Could a model be well calibrated but have poor discrimination?
  • Could a model have excellent discrimination but poor calibration?
  • What would systematic overprediction look like?
  • What would systematic underprediction look like?

Learning goal: Understand calibration as agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event frequencies.

Exercise 6A: Putting A, B, and C together

Two models are evaluated in the same external validation dataset.

Performance measure Model A Model B
AUC 0.88 0.78
Calibration Poor Excellent
Calibration slope 0.55 0.98

Questions:

  • Which model has better discrimination?
  • Which model gives more trustworthy absolute probabilities?
  • What does the calibration slope of 0.55 tell you about Model A?
  • Can you conclude that Model A is simply “better” because it has the higher AUC?
  • Can you conclude that Model B is simply “better” because it is better calibrated?
  • What additional information might you need before choosing between the models?
  • Could the preferred model depend on the intended use of the predictions?

Learning goal: Integrate discrimination, calibration, and clinical usefulness when evaluating predictive models rather than relying on a single performance measure.
