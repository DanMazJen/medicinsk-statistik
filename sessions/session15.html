16.2 The danger of coefficients

Warning

In nonlinear models, you cannot generally interpret the regression coefficient as the change in probability.

Important

The purpose of marginaleffects is essentially to help us make this translation: From a fitted generalized model → to quantities that answer the scientific question.

16.3 Marginalizing the problem - our workflow:

Throughout this session, we’ll use this workflow:

Step 1 — What is my scientific question?

What happens to the probability of Y when X changes?

Step 2 — What did the regression estimate?

log-odds

Step 3 — What quantity do I actually want to communicate?

probability

Step 4 — At what values?

  • A particular patient?
  • A clinically meaningful scenario?
  • The average population?
  • Different levels of an interacting variable?

Step 5 — Use marginal effects

avg_slopes(model)

or

slopes(model, …)

or

avg_comparisons(model, …)

Step 6 — Communicate in clinically meaningful units

Prefer:

1.8 percentage-point increase in predicted probability

over:

AME = 0.018

As shown in Figure 16.1, we move from the scientific question, through the model scale, to a marginal effect on the probability scale.

16.5 The scientific model

We model the probability of disease:

P(Disease = 1 | X)

using logistic regression:

\[ {logit}(P(Y = 1)) = \beta\_0 + \beta\_1X_1 + \beta\_2X_2 + \cdots \]

16.6 Clear communication

Suppose:

OR = 1.50

What does this mean?

A 1-unit increase in age is associated with a 50% increase in the odds of cardiovascular disease, holding the other variables constant.

But the more important question is:

Does this mean that a 1-year increase in age increases the probability of disease by 50 percentage points?

No.

An odds ratio describes a change in odds, not directly a change in probability.

16.6.1 Why the odds ratio is not enough

The clinical question is usually about risk/probability, not odds.

Consider two patients:

  • Patient A has a predicted disease probability of 5%.
  • Patient B has a predicted disease probability of 50%.

Suppose both experience the same change in the predictor.

The resulting change in probability will not necessarily be the same.

This is the key complication of logistic regression:

The effect of X on probability depends on where you start.

The relationship between X and probability is nonlinear.

The logistic curve is relatively flat near 0 and 1, and steepest in the middle.

Therefore:

There is not necessarily one single “effect of X” on probability.

16.6.2 What is a marginal effect?

A marginal effect asks:

How much does predicted probability change when X changes?

For a continuous variable:

What happens to predicted probability if X increases by 1 unit?

For example:

If age increases by 1 year, how much does predicted probability of disease change?

In a nonlinear model, this is calculated on the probability scale, rather than the log-odds scale.

With {marginaleffects}:

library(marginaleffects)

avg_slopes(model)

Conceptually, the output answers: A 1-unit increase in age → Δ predicted probability

If the result were:

age: 0.012

the interpretation would be:

A one-year increase in age is associated with a 1.2 percentage-point increase in predicted probability, on average.

Notice the distinction:

0.012 probability

1.2 percentage points

16.6.3 The effect depends on the patient

Suppose we want to know the effect of age for a particular patient.

We might ask:

What is the marginal effect of age for a 50-year-old non-smoker with normal blood pressure?

versus:

What is the marginal effect of age for a 75-year-old smoker with high blood pressure?

These can be different.

We can calculate effects for specified covariate values:

slopes(
  model,
  newdata = datagrid(
    age = c(50, 75),
    smoking = c(0, 1),
    blood_pressure = c(120, 160)
  )
)

The important concept is not the syntax.

It is:

A marginal effect is conditional on the characteristics of the person for whom we calculate it.

16.7 Predicted probabilities versus marginal effects

These are different questions.

Predicted probability What is this patient’s predicted probability of disease?

predictions(
  model,
  newdata = datagrid(
    age = 60,
    smoking = 0,
    blood_pressure = 130
  )
)

Marginal effect How much does the predicted probability change if age increases?

slopes(
  model,
  newdata = datagrid(
    age = 60,
    smoking = 0,
    blood_pressure = 130
  )
)

Predictions:

“What is the risk?”

Marginal effects:

“How much does the risk change?”

Both are useful, but they answer different scientific questions.

16.8 Average marginal effects

Often we do not want the effect for one particular patient.

We want:

What is the average effect in this population?

That is the average marginal effect (AME).

avg_slopes(model)

Conceptually:

Patient 1: effect of X = …
Patient 2: effect of X = …
Patient 3: effect of X = …
       ⋮
Patient N: effect of X = …

Average = AME

The interpretation is:

On average, a one-unit increase in X changes predicted probability by Δ percentage points.

This is often much easier to communicate than an odds ratio.

16.9 Interactions: when the effect depends on something else

Suppose our model includes:

Age × Smoking

The scientific question becomes:

Does the effect of age on disease probability differ between smokers and non-smokers?

The model might be:

model <- glm(
  disease ~ age * smoking + blood_pressure,
  data = data,
  family = binomial
)

The temptation is to look at the interaction coefficient and interpret it directly.

Don’t.

The interaction coefficient is on the log-odds scale.

Instead, calculate the marginal effect of age at different smoking statuses:

slopes(
  model,
  variables = "age",
  by = "smoking"
)

Now the result directly addresses the scientific question:

Effect of age among non-smokers Effect of age among smokers

And you can ask:

Is the difference between those marginal effects clinically meaningful?

16.10 Visualizing the interaction

plot_predictions(
  model,
  condition = c("age", "smoking")
)

16.11 Comparing clinically meaningful changes

A one-unit change is not always clinically meaningful.

For example, instead of asking:

What is the effect of a 1-unit increase in blood pressure?

we might ask:

What is the change in predicted probability when blood pressure increases from 120 to 140 mmHg?

For this, use a comparison:

avg_comparisons(
  model,
  variables = list(
    blood_pressure = c(120, 140)
  )
)

This produces a quantity directly corresponding to the clinical question:

Predicted probability at 140 − Predicted probability at 120 = change in probability

16.12 Uncertainty A marginal effect is an estimate, not a known quantity.

Therefore we also report uncertainty:

  • Estimate
  • 95% CI
  • p-value

For example:

AME = 0.018 (95% CI 0.010–0.026)

Interpretation:

On average, a one-unit increase in X is associated with a 1.8 percentage-point increase in predicted probability, with a 95% confidence interval from 1.0 to 2.6 percentage points.
